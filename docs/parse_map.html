What runs when you click parse

Two planes, drawn apart because they have opposite economics. Everything before atoms has a correct answer and is bought. Everything after is a judgment and is learned. Measured on 12 real documents / 374 pages, five of them public RFPs the parser had never seen — and, for the router section, on 2500 real artifacts renamed without touching a byte of their content.

PLANE ONE — BEFORE ATOMSPre-parse, routing, and decode

A file arrives. It is routed by its bytes, then every page is routed again by how much text it has — and that second routing is the fact that governs everything, because the three paths do completely different work.

What the whole-atom re-audit changed. The first pass searched raw_text and reported 24/33. Searching the entire atom, unmodified code already scores 44/49 — and with the surviving fixes, 49/49.

Two losses are real, and they share a shape: a plane-two judgement deciding what plane one is allowed to decode. The transcript gate is the clean example — “this utterance isn’t a decision” silently became “this utterance was never spoken”, leaving no atom, no receipt, nothing recoverable. The docx header/footer is simpler: a story part that was never opened.

The other two were mine. A collapse that nests its rows in value is not a deletion, and I called it one because I checked the wrong field. The lesson is the harness, not the parser: an audit that greps a display field measures the display, not the data.

The transcript number is the one that survived scrutiny. These utterances produced no atom in any field — not a summary, not a nested row, nothing. On a ten-turn call written in ordinary language the parser kept 5 of 10, and kept the wrong five.

Lost: “forty sites before the end of Q3” (the scope and the deadline) · “the dock is only open until two” (an access constraint) · “we are not paying for the mid-turn jumpers” (a commercial exclusion) · “Part 77-K298 … one hundred ninety two” (part number and quantity).

Kept: “Understood” · “Noted” · “Good”. The gate was keeping the chatter and discarding the content, because acknowledgements happen to contain the keywords. Now 10 of 10.

Two real defects, and one that turned out to be policy.

ICS discarded DESCRIPTION. It was parsed into the field dict and then never used — only summary, start, end, location and organiser reached the atom. On a real invite the summary is “Cutover window” and the description is “escort required at the dock, gate code 4512” plus the dial-in and the site contact. RFC 5545 unfolding was already correct, so the continuation lines were being reassembled and thrown away at the last step.

A ZIP member nobody could parse vanished. The extractor emitted a marker for a child that raised, and did a bare continue for a child with no parser — while the archive summary still counted it as extracted. That one outcome had no record anywhere. It leaves the same kind of marker now. The content is still unread, which is correct; the difference is the gap is auditable.

And .txt is claimed by no parser — deliberately. A plain notes.txt reaches nothing, and “Customer: Xtra Lease” in a readme is won by the transcript parser because the label reads as a speaker. That looked like a bug and is not: test_random_txt_produces_warning_no_crash requires an unrecognised .txt to produce “No parser matched artifact”. The warning is the coverage record — saying “I did not read this file” is more honest than parsing filler into atoms and implying nothing was there. The fix was reverted; the ZIP marker is that same principle applied one level down, where the report genuinely was missing because the ZIP is the artifact.

Four times the measuring instrument was the thing that was wrong. Worth recording, because each would have shipped as a false finding and two nearly did: reading raw_text instead of the whole atom (reported xlsx and csv dropping columns that were sitting in value["cells"]); a case-sensitive needle against a lowercased blob (reported the quote parser losing a vendor name it had captured); a fixture written with write_text on Windows, turning every \r\n into \r\r\n and breaking the very RFC 5545 folding it was testing; and pass/fail detection by string-matching pytest output, which reported a healthy test as failing 5 times out of 5.

On this codebase the instrument has been wrong more often than the code, and the tests encode reasoning that is not visible from the code alone.

The bbox join — how the two readers are married

The detector knows where content is and groups bullets and notes. Document Intelligence knows what each region is. Neither knows the other’s half, so they are joined on geometry.

mid-band pages only · 25% of pages

The fix that made it work. The first version measured only how much of the DI paragraph fell inside the box, assuming a box wraps a paragraph. Against the real detector that is often backwards:

DI  [499.2, 762.4, 596.7, 774.8]  ← the footer paragraph
box [535.2, 764.8, 598.4, 774.4]  ← the detected box, smaller

That scored 0.49 and was rejected, though the box sits almost entirely inside the paragraph. Dividing by the smaller area accepts both shapes and still rejects a pair that merely clips a corner. Result: 4 → 478 labels, 3 → 21 of 28 pages.

Where this has to be perfect — because no head can help here

Plane one cannot learn. There is no correction that teaches a router, so every decision it makes has to be right by construction. Measured across 374 pages, these are the places that are not.

decisiondecided byhow fragilefix without a head
Which of three pipelines a page takes length band, then page shape 11.5% of pages sit within ±10% of the 80-char line. A page with 72 chars is OCR’d; one with 88 goes to the box detector — different pipeline, different atoms, 16 characters apart. Route on whether extraction failed, not on a count: image-area ratio, or simply whether Document Intelligence recovered text where fitz recovered none.
Which reader wins on a page cells / lines recovered A count is a proxy for quality, not quality. It cannot tell 200 junk cells from 200 good ones. Adequate today because the gap is large (13 vs 964). Worth revisiting only if two readers ever come close.
Whether a line is page furniture DI role, then regex Was backwards before roles: stripped report.docx, kept the letterhead. Now correct on the DI path, still regex on the fitz fallback. Largely fixed. The regex remains only where DI did not run.
Where a claim came from receipts + locators Must be exact. This is the one thing a competitor cannot copy, and a wrong receipt is worse than no receipt. never learn — verified by replay, not predicted.

The page router's thresholds are the unvalidated part of plane one, and they are weak in a way that is invisible: nothing errors, a page just quietly goes down a pipeline built for a different kind of page. To be precise about what the router actually does — the earlier "text length only" label here was wrong — length picks the band, and inside the mid-band the decision consults image count and whether the page reads as multi-paragraph prose, a bulleted list or a form. The two outer bands are pure length, and that is where the sensitivity lives. 61 pages went to OCR and 95 to the box detector on this corpus — and 43 of them were within a rounding error of going somewhere else.

Characters-per-page also does not measure the thing that matters. The question is “did text extraction succeed, and does this page carry visual structure?” — and both are directly observable without predicting anything.

The service router, and what a base→head loop has to get right

This is the one router that is already a learned head, and it is the template for the rest — including the trap this project has already fallen into once.

Base → head · and the edge that must not exist

This has already happened here. The service router reported 91%. That number was DeepSeek reproducing its own labels: the head was trained on teacher labels and scored against teacher labels, so it measured how well it had imitated the teacher, which is not the question anyone was asking. No trustworthy accuracy for that router exists yet.

The fix is not a better head. It is a hold-out the teacher did not write — and the PM correction loop already produces exactly that, because a PM correcting a route is a label no teacher generated.

StepWhat runsWhat it is for
1LLM base routes in productionCorrect answers on day one, before any head exists. Cost is the price of the labels.
2Log the full input and the labelThe head must train on what production actually sees. Logging the label alone reproduces a decision nobody can re-derive.
3PM corrections accumulate separatelyThe hold-out. Small and slow, and the only labels not written by the thing being tested.
4Train the head on teacher labelsCheap, local, fast. Distillation is the easy half.
5Gate on the PM set onlyThe step that makes the number mean something. It has to be able to fail.
6Ship behind a flag, LLM on abstentionThe head answers what it knows and stands aside otherwise — which this router already does correctly via sim_floor.

The eval nobody has to build

A PM will never sit down and label a training set, and asking them to is how this stalls. They do not have to: the capture is already wired, and the labels are a byproduct of work they are doing anyway.

The chip payload already carries everything a label needs — head, old_value, new_value, the atom text, context, and pm — and confirm() writes a gold training row on the spot. Nothing new has to be built to collect. What is missing is the discipline about where those rows are allowed to go.

The rule that makes it an eval. Corrections currently feed the store, which fires them into the next compile. A row that trains the head cannot also score it. So the split has to be by deal, not by row: every correction from a held-out set of deals is eval-only and never reaches training.

Splitting row-wise leaks, and quietly. Corrections inside one deal are correlated — same customer vocabulary, same documents, same PM in the same hour — so a row-level split puts near-duplicates on both sides and hands back a number that looks like the circular one it replaced.

Never ask for a label. Record the decision they already make.

Every row below is something a PM does because it helps them, in the deal they are working, today. The label is a side effect. That is the only kind of labelling that survives contact with a deadline.

The label is not what the PM says. It is what the PM changes. Asking “was this route correct?” gets a shrug and a yes, because agreeing is faster than explaining. The diff between what was generated and what was sent does not have that problem — it is the PM's real opinion, expressed in the only currency they were already spending.

What this does not give you, said plainly

LimitWhy it mattersWhat to do about it
Biased toward errors a PM noticed A deal routed wrong that nobody caught never enters the set, so the score reads high for the same reason the circular one did — a different blind spot, not none. Rotate a small deliberate sample: a handful of random deals a month reviewed cold. It is the only line here that costs real minutes, so keep it small and keep it honest.
Slow to accumulate Corrections trickle. There is no month where this becomes a benchmark. Expect a hold-out in the tens for a long while. A small honest number beats a large circular one, and it can go down, which is the whole point.
Only covers deals that shipped Abandoned and stalled deals are exactly where routing may have failed hardest. Log the route on every compile, not every ship, so the population is visible even when the label is not.

What to do this quarter. Run the LLM base and spend the effort on the prompt — that is the right call while the head is untrustworthy. Two conditions make it compound instead of evaporate: log the full input next to every route, not just the label, or the prompt work leaves nothing trainable behind; and surface the router chip where the PM already notices the deal opened on the wrong pack, so the hold-out starts filling from day one rather than from the day someone decides to build it.

Two routers exist in parser-os, and they are not a pair. app/domain/pack_router.py routes deals deterministically — project.yaml, then SOURCE_NOTES, then filename heuristics, then content scoring. But it emits DOMAIN packs (copper_cabling, security_camera) while the service router emits SERVICE packs (wireless, audio_visual, low_voltage_cabling). The vocabularies overlap and the label spaces do not, so pairing them would have produced a disagreement rate that measured nothing. They are not paired.

The shadow harness already existed too. app/core/shadow_eval.py does leave-one-deal-out scoring, separates coverage from accuracy, and reports gold accuracy on PM-taught rows apart from the rest — written for the extractor relations and never pointed at the router. What the new evaluation adds is the fail-closed human check, the refusals, and the paired comparison against the shipping router.

Which is this whole session's finding one more time: the machinery was right, and something upstream of it had never been connected.

A3 These are teacher-agreement numbers — the holdout labels are DeepSeek's. For the deflect slot that is the correct objective (reproduce the LLM without calling it). For correctness it is not: on the 23 PM-gold holdout rows the head answered 8 and got 4 — below any honest reporting threshold, but the misses are taxonomy-adjacent pairs (contract_term/payment_term) the teacher itself likely confuses. 82% agreement ≠ 82% correct. A4 Conformal saturated informatively. qhat = 1.0: for ≥10% of calibration rows the true label is entirely absent from the k=15 neighbourhood — the base encoder cannot reach the tail classes. And measured coverage (84.5%) dipped under the 90% guarantee, because deal-clustered rows are not exchangeable. qhat is now the metric the GO #5 fine-tune has to move: pull the tail into reachable neighbourhoods. The exchangeability dip says: calibrate on more, smaller deal groups as the corpus grows.

The outcome: a candidate that refused itself. The cascade's contrastive slot learned a "typed" mode — value-light fine types assigned at the strict gate (tau=0.98, the measured 99% line), guess-free fallthrough, flag-off byte-identical, pinned by a test that booby-traps the loader. build_atom_store.py materialised the candidate from the real table and evaluated it during its own build: 167 strict answers at 97.6% overall — but only 7 of 358 value-light-eligible rows answered, at 71%, because the head's confident classes (bom_line 94%, commercial_total 94%) are exactly the value-heavy ones the cascade reserves for the LLM, which synthesises their value payloads. So the artifact wrote ready: false into its own meta, with the numbers and the caveats inside it.

That is the anti-gold_acc=1.0 shape working on day one: a candidate that cannot describe itself cannot be promoted, and this one describes itself as not ready. It also converts GO #5 from a wish into a single-metric mission: move value_light_answered off 7. The trainer has its table, its target, and its incumbent. And the measurement audited itself twice on the way — the first conformal line read 100%/100% because the true label had leaked into the test-time scores; at inference nobody knows the truth. Fixed, re-run, and the honest lines are the ones above.

The smoke run passed by refusing. A 400-row, 1-epoch CPU fit made the encoder worse (1@0.000 vs the frozen baseline’s 15@0.133) and the trainer’s last line was “no epoch beat the frozen baseline — NOTHING WRITTEN.” That is the machinery working: the same gate that will stop a bad A100 run from shipping stopped a deliberately-starved CPU run from shipping. The loop is proven; only the checkpoint quality awaits real data and real silicon.

When the GPU is actually worth renting. Everything live today — parsing, routing, reconciliation, instant-learning corrections, the conformal gate — is CPU work. The one thing that needs a GPU is moving the encoder’s weights: ~6,200 SupCon steps over 16.5k rows is roughly 3.5 days on this CPU and ~30 minutes on an A100. But a run today would optimise agreement with a machine teacher on v0 text while serving is v2 — a sharper wrong answer. Each PM thumb fixes both defects at once: correctness-grade label, v2 representation. The box gets rented when pm-gold is big enough to arbitrate (≈100 taps), and the weekend cadence — train Saturday, McNemar Sunday, promote or discard — is what the trainer’s gate already implements.

Two days of work the map did not yet show: the discovery that the deployed service was silently eating every correction, and the audit campaign that followed the parsers’ own discipline through every remaining layer of this repo.

The training data was graded and failed, and the grading found a live leak. The “218 PM-gold rows” turned out to be rank-laundered pipeline rows with empty provenance — verified human labels across every asset: zero.
